Questions

  1. Daily vs annual. With κ fixed, switch between Daily and Annual. By what factor does the skin depth change? Is it the same factor as the ratio of the periods?
    Hint d grows with the square root of the period. A year is about 365 days, so d grows by roughly 365 ≈ 19.
  2. Doubling the period. Choose Custom, then double the period, then quadruple it. What happens to d?
    Hint Doubling the period multiplies d by 2 ≈ 1.41; quadrupling it doubles d.
  3. The delayed wave. Move the probe depth to about π skin depths. When the surface is hottest, what is the temperature change at the probe? How big is the cycle there compared with the surface?
    Hint At z = πd the wave is half a cycle behind, so the ground is at its coldest when the surface is at its hottest. Only e−π ≈ 4% of the amplitude is left.
  4. Does the gradient matter? Change the geothermal gradient. Which of the key numbers change, and which do not? Why?
    Hint None of the skin depth, amplitude, or lag numbers change. Heat conduction is linear, so the periodic part and the steady geotherm simply add.
  5. Where to measure a gradient. Choose Annual with an amplitude of 10 °C. At what depth has the seasonal cycle shrunk below 0.1 °C? Why should a geothermal gradient be measured below that depth?
    Hint A e−z/d = 0.1 °C gives z = d ln(100) ≈ 4.6 d, about 15 m for κ = 10−6 m²/s. Above that depth the seasonal wave would distort the measured gradient.
  6. Ice-age signals. Choose 100 kyr and tick “Show only the deviation.” How deep does the signal reach? Compare that with the depth of a typical borehole (hundreds of metres to a couple of kilometres).
    Hint d is about 1 km, so the signal is still noticeable at several kilometres. This is why deep borehole temperature profiles can carry a record of past surface temperature.

The model

Equation, symbols, and assumptions

The ground is a uniform half-space (depth z downward) whose surface temperature oscillates as a sine wave. Once any start-up transient has died away, the temperature is the steady geotherm plus a damped, delayed wave:

T(z, t) = T0 + G z + A e−z/d cos(2πt/P − z/d),   d = κP/π
  • T0: mean surface temperature. G: geothermal gradient. A: surface amplitude. P: period.
  • κ: thermal diffusivity, the ratio of thermal conductivity to volumetric heat capacity.
  • d: skin depth. The amplitude falls by a factor of e at depth d and the wave lags the surface by z/d radians, which is (z/d) P/2π in time.

Assumptions:

  • Heat moves by conduction only and κ is the same everywhere.
  • The surface temperature is a sine wave (real daily, seasonal and glacial cycles are not, but a real cycle can be built from several sine waves of different periods).
  • The gradient is constant with depth (no radiogenic heat production, no layering).
  • Ground-surface temperature is not the same as air temperature (snow, vegetation, and soil moisture may change their relationship).

See: Turcotte & Schubert, Geodynamics, Chapter 4 or Fowler, The Solid Earth, Chapter 7.

Derivation

We solve the one-dimensional diffusion equation in a half-space with harmonic surface forcing, in the periodic steady state. Complex notation reduces the problem to an ODE in z.

  1. Diffusion equation

    Energy conservation for a slab between z and z + dz, with density ρ, specific heat capacity c and downward heat flux q(z, t), gives

    ρc dz ∂T∂t = q(z) − q(z + dz)
     = −∂q∂z dz

    Fourier’s law, with thermal conductivity k, gives

    q = −k ∂T∂z

    Substituting, with k, ρ and c uniform, yields the diffusion equation with thermal diffusivity κ = k/(ρc):

    ∂T∂t = κ ∂²T∂z², 
    κ = k/(ρc)    (1)

    The steady geotherm T0 + Gz carries a uniform upward heat flux kG, the surface heat flow.

  2. Boundary conditions

    At the surface the temperature is prescribed as a harmonic oscillation about its mean T0:

    T(0, t) = T0 + A cos ωt, 
    ω = 2π/P    (2)

    At depth the solution must approach the undisturbed geotherm T0 + Gz:

    T(z, t) → (T0 + G z)  
    as z → ∞    (3)

    G is set by the heat flux from the deep interior, which the surface forcing does not alter. No initial condition is imposed: we seek the periodic steady state, which the solution approaches once the transient from the initial condition has decayed.

  3. Subtracting the geotherm

    The geotherm is independent of t and linear in z, so it solves (1) on its own. Write

    T(z, t) = T0 + G z + θ(z, t)

    By linearity, θ also satisfies (1). At z = 0 the geotherm reduces to T0, so (2) becomes θ(0, t) = A cos ωt, and (3) becomes θ → 0:

    ∂θ∂t = κ ∂²θ∂z²
    θ(0, t) = A cos ωt
    θ → 0 as z → ∞    (4)

    θ is independent of G and T0. In other words, the oscillation is independent of the background gradient.

  4. Harmonic ansatz

    Write the forcing as A cos ωt = Re(A eiωt). Since (4) is linear with time-independent coefficients, the periodic steady state responds at the forcing frequency:

    θ(z, t) = Re[ Θ(z) eiωt ]

    where the modulus and argument of the complex amplitude Θ(z) give the amplitude and phase at depth z. Substituting into (4) and cancelling eiωt gives

    iω Θ = κ d²Θdz²
    ⇒ d²Θdz² = β² Θ, 
    β² = iωκ    (5)
  5. Solving for the depth dependence

    The general solution of (5) is

    Θ(z) = C1 eβz + C2 e−βz

    Using the principal square root of i,

    i = eiπ/2 = eiπ/4 = 1 + i2

    the root of β² = iω/κ with positive real part is

    β = (1 + i) ω/2κ
    = 1 + id, 
    d = 2κ/ω    (6)

    Since Re β = 1/d > 0, the eβz term grows without bound and violates (3), so C1 = 0. The surface condition Θ(0) = A fixes C2 = A:

    Θ(z) = A e−βz
    = A e−z/d e−iz/d    (7)
  6. Real part

    Taking the real part of Θ eiωt gives

    θ = Re[ A e−z/d ei(ωt − z/d) ]
    = A e−z/d cos(ωt − z/d)

    Restoring the geotherm and writing d in terms of the period (ω = 2π/P) gives the result used throughout this page:

    T(z, t) = T0 + G z
    + A e−z/d cos(2πt/P − z/d),
    d = 2κ/ω = κP/π    (8)

    Check. With φ = ωt − z/d, differentiation gives ∂θ/∂t = −ωA e−z/d sin φ and ∂²θ/∂z² = −(2/d²) A e−z/d sin φ. Since d² = 2κ/ω, κ ∂²θ/∂z² = ∂θ/∂t, so (4) holds. Also θ(0, t) = A cos ωt, and θ → 0 as z → ∞.

  7. Properties of the solution
    • Attenuation. The amplitude decays as e−z/d: it falls to 1/e at the skin depth d and to 1% at z = d ln 100 ≈ 4.6 d. This sets the shaded band in the temperature–depth figure.
    • Phase. The phase lag is z/d radians, a delay of (z/d) P/2π, which is the lag marked in the time-series figure. The wave propagates downward with phase speed ωd and wavelength 2πd. At z = πd it is perfectly out of phase with the surface, with e−π ≈ 4.3% of the surface amplitude.
    • Scaling. d = κP/π ∝ P, the diffusive scaling d² ∼ κP. By linearity, any periodic forcing is a Fourier sum of such terms, each with its own d.
